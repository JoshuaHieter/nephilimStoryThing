# The Crossing

> How you travel from Earth to the other worlds: sail far from land, a strange star appears, sail toward it, and you cross.

## Overview

The only known way between worlds. **To humans** it appears to be blind chance — it happens to ships that get **far enough from any land**. When they do, **special stars appear** that weren't there before — **one for each other world**. Sail *toward* one of them and you cross over to that world. But that randomness is a human limitation, not the truth of it — see [[#A real method — that humans don't have]].

## The crossing, step by step

1. **Sail out of sight of all land.** Once you are far enough from any landmass that you **can no longer see it**, new stars appear.
2. **The world-stars appear — one for each other world.** Each reachable world has its own star. They sit in a **fixed part of the sky**, unmoving — the **same position no matter the time of night or where you are on your world** (unlike the ordinary stars, which wheel as normal). Count the world-stars and you've counted the worlds ([[The Known Worlds]]).
3. **Sail deliberately toward one — intent matters.** You must steer **directly at your chosen star, meaning to do it.** *(The intent is to sail toward the star, not necessarily to cross — which is how people cross "by accident": they chase the star without knowing where it leads. See [[The Pirate]].)*
4. **You enter "sea limbo."** *(Working name.)* After a while you pass into a between-place: **no world beneath you, just open sea.** You are nowhere — in transit.
5. **The sky swaps over.** As you press on toward your star, the **ordinary stars of the world you left slowly wink out**, while the **stars of the world you're going to wink in.** The heavens change over you as you cross.
6. **Land = arrival.** The sea itself never looks any different — so the one sign you've truly **arrived is when you sight landmass.** That landfall means your ship is now **physically on the destination world**, and only here do you begin to meet its **biology and ecology** — its strange life and [[The Known Worlds|monstrous seas]].

### Steering in sea limbo

Sea limbo isn't a trap, and it isn't quite featureless:

- **You can turn back.** You're free to change course in the between-sea.
- **You exit onto whichever world's star you're most closely pointed toward** — not necessarily where you came from. To return *home*, you have to aim at your **origin world's star.**
- **The closer you point dead-on at a star, the faster you arrive.** A precise heading gets you there quickly; a loose or oblique one drags the crossing out.

### For humans, specifically

- **Mostly one-way.** Ships have crossed by accident before, but **almost no one comes back.**
- **Survival is luck, not skill.** The few who returned simply **turned the right way and lived** — see [[The Voodoo Pirates]]. No human knows a ritual, bloodline, or craft that reliably crosses or returns. *(The Nephilim do — see below.)*

## You don't need a big ship

The trigger is only **getting out of sight of land** (step 1) — *not* crossing an ocean. So in truth a **small boat is enough:** get a skiff beyond the horizon, aim at your star, and cross. The danger was never the *distance* — it's **ignorance** (wrong star, lost in sea limbo) and the **monster-seas** you arrive in.

- **Proof:** [[The Outcast Scholar]] crossed to Earth **alone, in a tiny boat** (he knew the method; he died of poor seamanship and the crash, not the crossing).
- **But the [[The Pope's Mission|mission]] didn't know that.** Reverse-engineering the diary, they assumed a long ocean voyage and brought a **full ship and crew** — a reasonable mistake. (The monsters thinned them anyway.)
- **It's how two survivors get home:** a stolen skiff, [[The Captain|Drake]]'s seamanship, and [[The Lutheran|Konrad]]'s cracked method are all it takes — see [[Book Plotline]].

## A real method — that humans don't have

The randomness is a **human limitation, not the nature of the crossing.** There is an **actual, learnable method** to crossing reliably — and, it follows, to steering where you go and getting back.

- **Until recently, humans had no idea it even existed.** To them it was all luck.
- **The Nephilim know it — and to them it's nothing special.** ([[The Nephilim Worlds]].) Their escape from the Flood began as an accident, but they **learned how it works** long ago. Now it's simply **how the other worlds travel between one another** — routine, unremarkable, the kind of thing you mention in passing. It's no rare feat: it happens **about as often as travel between continents does on Earth** — established and ordinary, but a real voyage, not an everyday hop. See [[Nephilim & the Flood]], [[The Known Worlds]].
- **Even among the Nephilim, the *specifics* are scholarly knowledge.** Only learned Nephilim know about **sea limbo**, the winking stars, and the rules of heading and speed. **Most Nephilim just think you sail toward the special star, and that's all there is to it** — the way most people use a tool without grasping its inner workings. ([[The Outcast Scholar]] was such a scholar — which is why his diary could be mined for the method at all.)

### The breakthrough: a stolen, half-finished method

This is changing — because of a dead Nephilim. [[The Outcast Scholar|His diary]], written in something close to ancient Hebrew, has let human scholars **reverse-engineer how to make the crossing.** For the first time, a *deliberate* human crossing is possible.

But the stolen knowledge is **partial**: it covers **getting *there*, not getting *back*.** Going is a choice; the **return is unsolved** — they don't know which star is Earth's, or how to steer home through sea limbo. That's why the [[The Pope's Mission]] can set out on purpose and still end up **stranded.** *(Only later, on the far side, does [[The Lutheran|Konrad]] crack the return — which is how the two survivors get home; see [[Book Plotline]].)*

## A death sentence — and why no crew will sail at the star

The crossing isn't deadly in itself — for the Nephilim it's routine. It's deadly to the **ignorant.** Without the method, a human crossing is **near-certain death and effectively one-way**, so sailors know to **fear the star, not chase it.** To steer *toward* it not knowing how to survive it is to sail your ship and everyone aboard into the grave.

- **No ordinary crew will knowingly do it.** Faced with the star, a sane crew **refuses, mutinies, or flees** rather than follow it.
- Only the **desperate, the doomed, or the fanatical** sail at the star on purpose.
- The [[The Pope's Mission]] gets a crew past this dread only through the **Pope's command** and faith in the **reverse-engineered method** — enough to make hardened men sail at the star. The star doesn't kill them; **what waits in the far world's seas does.**

## Where it leads

Other worlds — possibly other **planets**, possibly other **dimensions**. See [[The Known Worlds]] for their structure, and [[Nephilim & the Flood]] for who fled there long ago. Note: each world is mostly uninhabitable; **the star lands you at the one near-normal-habitat spot.**

## Open questions

- Other **planets** or other **dimensions**? (Undecided — though "sea limbo" and the swapping sky hint at something stranger than plain distance.)
- **"Sea limbo"** — keep the name? And what *is* it: a true nowhere, or a place of its own, with its own dangers?
- Can you **turn back or get lost** mid-crossing? What happens if you lose your star, or change your mind, in sea limbo?
- Why does **intent** matter — what exactly counts as "meaning to" sail toward the star?
